WebRTC, actually understood.

01 — why it's hard

You cannot just open a socket

TL;DRConnecting two browsers fails for four independent reasons at once. Each reason is the seed of a whole WebRTC subsystem.

Your first instinct is socket.connect(ip, port). It dies instantly — and the four ways it dies are the map of everything that follows.

force 1

You can't find the peer

You don't know their address, and even if you did, NAT makes it unreachable.

→ ICE · STUN · TURN
force 2

You can't introduce them

Two strangers behind NAT can't reach each other to even begin. Someone must broker it.

→ signaling · SDP
force 3

TCP is the wrong pipe

Reliable, ordered delivery is poison for live media. UDP is right but gives you nothing.

→ DTLS · SRTP · SCTP
force 4

The network fights back

Packets drop, bandwidth swings, paths jitter. You must adapt every second.

→ RTP · RTCP · GCC

WebRTC isn't one protocol — it's a dozen older ones (mostly from the 2000s VoIP world) bolted together behind a browser API. The rest of this page takes the four forces in order of how fundamental they are.

02 — transport

Why UDP, not TCP

TL;DRFor live media a late packet is worse than a lost one. TCP makes everything wait for a lost packet; UDP lets you skip it. WebRTC builds its own reliability on UDP — exactly as much as it needs.

TCP guarantees in-order, reliable delivery. To do that it has a receive buffer: if packet #3 is lost but #4, #5, #6 arrive, TCP refuses to hand them to your app until #3 is retransmitted. That's head-of-line blocking. For a download it's correct; for a live call it's a disaster — by the time the resent #3 arrives a round-trip later, the moment is gone. Press the button and watch the two transports diverge.

UDP — fire and forgetskips #3

TCP holds packets 4–6 hostage until the lost one is retransmitted. UDP plays everything that arrived and accepts a tiny glitch where #3 should be.
the central truth

For real-time media, freshness beats completeness — because a late packet also delays everything queued behind it.

So WebRTC builds on UDP: no ordering, no retransmission, no congestion control, no connection state. That blankness is the feature. WebRTC adds back precisely the reliability each stream needs — selective retransmission and FEC for video, full reliability for a file transfer — and nothing it doesn't.

▸ Under the hood — what UDP actually is, and the TCP costs beyond head-of-line

UDP adds an 8-byte header to an IP packet: source port, destination port, length, checksum. That's the entire protocol — it's a thin demultiplexing shim so the OS knows which socket a datagram belongs to. No sequence numbers, no acknowledgements, no windows, no state machine.

TCP hurts live media in three ways, not one. Head-of-line blocking is the headline. But TCP's congestion control (slow-start, AIMD) is tuned for bulk throughput and reacts to a single loss by halving its window, which crushes a video bitrate. And TCP induces bufferbloat: it keeps the pipe full, so routers queue its bytes and add tens to hundreds of milliseconds of standing latency. Media wants the opposite — keep queues empty, keep delay low, tolerate a little loss.

When UDP is completely blocked, WebRTC falls back to TCP — usually tunnelled through a TURN server on port 443 with TLS, so it looks like ordinary HTTPS to a firewall. It's the unhappy path: you accept TCP's latency penalties because the alternative is no connection at all.

03 — connectivity

NAT, and the four flavors that decide your fate

TL;DRYour device shares one public IP via NAT and never learns its own public address. STUN teaches it. TURN relays when STUN can't. ICE tries all of them and keeps what works.

IPv4 ran out of addresses, so NAT lets a whole home share one public IP. Your router rewrites your private 192.168.1.5:50000 to 1.2.3.4:62000 on the way out and reverses it on replies. This breaks peer-to-peer three ways: you never learn your own public mapping, your private address is meaningless on the internet, and the router drops any inbound packet that doesn't match a connection you started.

analogy

NAT is an office receptionist. Outsiders can't dial your desk directly; they reach the front desk, which routes calls it's expecting. The kind of receptionist you have — how strict they are about who's allowed to call back — is your NAT type, and it decides whether a direct connection is even possible.

There are four classic types, increasingly strict about who may send to a mapping:

NAT typeWho can send back to your mappingDirect P2P?
full coneanyone, once the mapping existseasy
restricted coneonly IPs you've sent toworks (both punch)
port-restrictedonly the exact IP:port you've sent toworks (both punch)
symmetrica new mapping per destination — STUN's answer is wrongfails → relay

The villain is symmetric NAT: it hands out a different public port for every destination, so the address STUN reported (for the STUN server) isn't the one your peer will hit. That single behavior is why TURN relays must exist.

▸ Under the hood — the modern framing: mapping vs filtering behavior

The classic four-type taxonomy (RFC 3489) is convenient but imprecise. The modern model (RFC 4787 / 5780) splits a NAT's behavior into two independent axes. Mapping behavior: does the NAT reuse the same external port for the same internal socket regardless of destination (endpoint-independent), or allocate a new one per destination (address-and-port-dependent — the “symmetric” case)? Filtering behavior: which external senders are allowed to reach an existing mapping (endpoint-independent, address-restricted, or address-and-port-restricted)?

Hole punching works whenever mappings are endpoint-independent, because the address STUN observes is the address the peer will use. It fails when mapping is address-dependent, because the prediction is wrong. Carrier-grade NAT (CGNAT) used by mobile carriers is frequently address-dependent, which is why mobile-to-mobile calls relay more often than you'd hope.

STUN, TURN, ICE — in one breath

STUN is a public server that replies “I see you at 1.2.3.4:62000,” teaching you your public mapping (your server-reflexive candidate) and punching a hole at the same time. TURN is a public relay both peers reach outbound; it shovels packets between them when direct fails — always works, but adds a hop and costs bandwidth. ICE is the orchestrator: it gathers every candidate (host, reflexive, relay), exchanges them, fires connectivity checks across every pair, and nominates the highest-priority pair that actually works.

▸ Under the hood — candidate priority math, agent roles, trickle, and ICE restart

Candidate types, best to worst: host (your local interface address), server-reflexive / srflx (your public mapping via STUN), peer-reflexive / prflx (discovered mid-check when a packet arrives from an unexpected address), and relay (via TURN). Priority is computed so type dominates:

// RFC 8445 priority
priority = (2^24)*typePref + (2^8)*localPref + (256 - componentID)
// typePref: host=126, prflx=110, srflx=100, relay=0

Roles. One agent is controlling (usually the offerer), the other controlled. Both run checks, but the controlling agent decides which succeeding pair to nominate as the final path. Connectivity checks are STUN Binding requests carried over each candidate pair, authenticated with the ice-ufrag/ice-pwd from the SDP.

Trickle ICE sends candidates as they're discovered instead of waiting to gather them all, so checks start in parallel and connection time drops from seconds to a few hundred milliseconds. ICE restart generates fresh credentials and re-gathers when the path breaks (you switch from Wi-Fi to cellular), migrating the call without dropping it. ICE-lite is a stripped mode for servers with public IPs (like SFUs) that only respond to checks rather than gathering their own.

05 — signaling

The hole left in the spec on purpose

TL;DRWebRTC refuses to define how peers exchange setup info. You move a blob of text between them however you like; WebRTC handles everything after it arrives.

If two peers can't reach each other yet, how do they swap setup info? Through signaling — and WebRTC deliberately doesn't specify it. Every app already has a way to push data to clients (a WebSocket, HTTP, a chat backend), so mandating one (like SIP) would be dead weight. The rule: you get this text from A to B by any means — WebSocket, QR code, copy-paste — and WebRTC takes over from there.

analogy

Signaling is mailing someone your phone number and a list of languages you speak, before the call. Once you both have each other's details, you hang up the mail and talk directly. The post office (your server) never hears the conversation.

Two things travel over signaling: session descriptions (the Offer/Answer) and ICE candidates. Alice's offer says “here are my tracks, codecs, ICE credentials, and DTLS fingerprint”; Bob's answer is the intersection of what they both support. Both sides agree on everything before a media packet flows — a contract, written in SDP.

07 — security

DTLS-SRTP, and the fingerprint trick

TL;DREncryption is mandatory. Peers agree keys directly with a DTLS handshake; the signaling server only ever sees a fingerprint, never the keys.

There is no unencrypted WebRTC media — by spec. The instant ICE picks a path, the peers run a DTLS handshake across it (DTLS is TLS rebuilt for unreliable datagrams). The keys it produces are exported to key SRTP, which encrypts every media packet. This pairing is DTLS-SRTP: DTLS does key agreement, SRTP does bulk encryption.

why not put keys in the SDP?

Because SDP passes through your signaling server, which could then decrypt the call. So DTLS exchanges keys directly between peers; the SDP carries only a fingerprint (a hash of each certificate). After the handshake, each side checks the peer's real certificate against that fingerprint. The signaling plane authenticates, the media plane encrypts, and the fingerprint binds the two — so the server brokers the introduction but can never listen in.

▸ Under the hood — handshake roles, forward secrecy, and SRTP profiles

The SDP line a=setup:actpass negotiates DTLS roles: the offerer says it can be client or server, the answerer picks (active = it initiates the handshake as client, passive = server). Roles are independent of who made the call.

DTLS uses ephemeral (EC)DHE key exchange, giving perfect forward secrecy: recording the encrypted media and later stealing a long-term key still doesn't decrypt past sessions, because the session keys were ephemeral. The negotiated SRTP protection profile (e.g. AES-128-GCM or the older AES-CM-HMAC-SHA1) determines the cipher and authentication tag used per packet. Media isn't tunnelled inside DTLS — that would add per-packet overhead — DTLS only does the handshake, and SRTP encrypts datagrams directly using the exported keys.

08 — codecs

What's actually inside the packets

TL;DROpus encodes the audio, a video codec encodes the picture as one full keyframe plus tiny “what changed” deltas. Lose a delta's ancestor and the picture smears until a fresh keyframe arrives.

Before media can travel it must be compressed. Both peers list the codecs they support in the SDP and use the best one they share.

codecrolewhy you'd use it
Opusaudio (default)6–510 kbps, adapts live, superb quality and low latency; the universal choice
VP8video baselineroyalty-free, supported everywhere — the safe default
VP9videobetter compression, supports scalable layers (SVC)
H.264videobroad hardware encode/decode, interop with phones and legacy gear
AV1video (newest)best compression by far, at a higher CPU cost

The crucial idea for understanding loss: video is mostly delta frames. A keyframe (I-frame) is a complete picture — large. Every frame after it is a delta (P-frame) encoding only what changed — tiny. That's the compression win, but it's also fragile: lose the packets for one frame and every delta built on it decodes into garbage, so the receiver must ask for a fresh keyframe to recover. That request is the RTCP PLI you'll meet next.

▸ Under the hood — why Opus dominates, and how SVC differs from simulcast

Opus is really two codecs fused (SILK for speech, CELT for music) with a controller that crossfades between them by content and bitrate. It does in-band FEC and packet-loss concealment, ships frames as short as 2.5–20 ms for low latency, and changes bitrate per packet — so it degrades gracefully instead of cutting out.

Simulcast means the sender encodes the same video several independent times at different resolutions and ships all of them; an SFU forwards whichever stream suits each receiver. SVC (scalable video coding, e.g. in VP9/AV1) encodes one stream in nested layers — a base layer plus enhancement layers — so the SFU can drop the top layers per receiver without re-encoding. SVC is more efficient and more robust (the base layer alone still decodes) but needs codec support; simulcast works with any codec at the cost of extra uplink.

09 — media plane

RTP carries it; RTCP makes it adapt

TL;DRRTP tags every packet with a sequence number and timestamp so the receiver can detect loss and time playout. RTCP is the back-channel where the receiver reports trouble and the sender reacts.

Each RTP packet carries four things that matter: a sequence number (detect loss and reordering), a timestamp (playout timing, lip-sync, jitter buffer), an SSRC (which stream this is), and a payload type (which codec). RTP adds no reliability — it adds the metadata you need to handle unreliability on purpose.

Its sibling RTCP is what turns “sends video” into “sends video that survives a real network.” The receiver continuously reports loss, jitter and round-trip time, and sends targeted control messages back. The sender reacts in a closed loop:

RTCP message types you'll actually see: Receiver/Sender Reports (periodic loss %, jitter, last-timestamp for RTT); NACK (“I'm missing sequence numbers 1200–1203, resend”); PLI / FIR (“my decoder is broken, send a keyframe”); REMB (older receiver-estimated max bitrate) and transport-cc (newer per-packet arrival feedback that lets the sender estimate bandwidth). RTCP traffic is capped at roughly 5% of the media bandwidth so feedback never starves the media itself. With a=rtcp-mux, RTCP shares the single RTP port instead of using a second one — fewer NAT holes to punch.

10 — surviving loss

Three ways to beat packet loss

TL;DRAsk for a resend (NACK) when there's time, send redundant data ahead (FEC) when there isn't, and request a fresh keyframe (PLI) when the picture is already broken. A jitter buffer smooths the rest.

UDP loses packets; WebRTC has a toolbox, each tool a different point on the latency-versus-bandwidth tradeoff:

toolhowcost
NACK + RTXreceiver asks for the exact lost packet; sender resends itone round-trip — only worth it if RTT is small
FECsend redundant repair data ahead of time so loss is reconstructed locallyextra bandwidth, but zero added latency
PLI / FIRgive up on the broken frame, request a brand-new keyframea bandwidth spike + a brief quality dip

After recovery, packets still arrive at uneven intervals (network jitter). The jitter buffer holds them briefly, reorders them, and releases them on a steady clock — trading a little latency for smooth playback. Bigger buffer = smoother but laggier; smaller = snappier but glitchier. WebRTC sizes it adaptively.

The delay-based controller targets a bitrate just under the estimated capacity. Loss penalizes the estimate faster than bandwidth alone, because loss signals a pipe already overflowing.

This is why your face goes blocky for a second on a bad network and then sharpens — you're watching the control loop find a new target and the encoder follow it.

▸ Under the hood — Google Congestion Control, delay gradient vs loss

GCC runs two controllers. A delay-based controller (at the receiver, or at the sender with transport-cc feedback) measures the inter-group delay variation — if packets sent close together arrive increasingly spread apart, a queue is forming, so it backs the estimate down before any loss occurs. A loss-based controller adjusts in parallel: under ~2% loss it nudges the rate up, above ~10% it cuts hard. The final send rate is the minimum of the two, so whichever signal is more pessimistic wins. The encoder then targets that rate by trading resolution, frame rate, and quantization.

12 — data

The data channel rides the same wire

TL;DRArbitrary data reuses the same encrypted connection over SCTP, which lets you dial reliability and ordering per channel — from TCP-like to UDP-like.

For non-media data — chat, game state, file transfer — WebRTC reuses the same ICE path and DTLS encryption, but runs SCTP inside. Why SCTP and not TCP or raw UDP? Because SCTP gives you a dial:

modebehaves likegood for
reliable + orderedTCPfile transfer, chat history
unreliable + unorderedUDPfast game state where stale data is worthless
partially reliablein between“retransmit, but give up after 3 tries or 100 ms”

It's also message-oriented (TCP is a raw byte stream you'd have to frame yourself) and avoids head-of-line blocking across independent streams. The same “exactly the reliability you need, no more” philosophy from the very first UDP decision — now handed to you, the app developer.

▸ Under the hood — the four separate state machines

connectionState is an aggregate. Underneath it are three others you can watch independently. signalingState tracks the offer/answer dance (stable → have-local-offer → stable). iceGatheringState tracks candidate discovery (new → gathering → complete). iceConnectionState tracks the ICE checks (new → checking → connected → completed, or disconnected/failed). A disconnected ICE state is often transient — a few lost checks — and may recover on its own or via ICE restart; failed means no candidate pair worked at all and you need fresh candidates.

14 — in code

The whole thing, in ~20 lines

TL;DRThe browser API hides almost everything. You create a peer connection, add tracks, shuttle the offer/answer and candidates through your own signaling, and the engine does ICE, DTLS, RTP for you.

Everything on this page reduces to a small, legible API. Here's a complete caller side, annotated:

// 1. the engine. iceServers tell ICE where STUN/TURN live.
const pc = new RTCPeerConnection({
  iceServers: [
    { urls: 'stun:stun.l.google.com:19302' },
    { urls: 'turn:turn.example.com', username: 'u', credential: 'p' }
  ]
});

// 2. capture camera + mic, attach each track to the connection
const stream = await navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia({ audio:true, video:true });
stream.getTracks().forEach(t => pc.addTrack(t, stream));

// 3. trickle our ICE candidates to the peer as they're found
pc.onicecandidate = e => { if (e.candidate) signaling.send({ candidate: e.candidate }); };

// 4. when remote media arrives, show it
pc.ontrack = e => { remoteVideo.srcObject = e.streams[0]; };

// 5. make the offer, set it locally (this starts ICE), send it
const offer = await pc.createOffer();
await pc.setLocalDescription(offer);
signaling.send({ sdp: pc.localDescription });

// 6. as signaling messages come back from the peer:
signaling.onmessage = async (m) => {
  if (m.sdp)        await pc.setRemoteDescription(m.sdp);   // the answer
  if (m.candidate)  await pc.addIceCandidate(m.candidate);  // their candidates
};

The answerer is the mirror image: on the incoming offer it calls setRemoteDescription, then createAnswer → setLocalDescription → send back. A data channel is two more lines: const dc = pc.createDataChannel('chat'), then dc.onmessage and dc.send(...). Notice what you never write: NAT logic, key exchange, retransmission, bitrate control. That's all the engine.

17 — the honest caveat

Pure P2P only scales to small calls

TL;DRGroup calls don't mesh — a server called an SFU sits in the middle and forwards streams. It still speaks WebRTC, so everything here still applies.

In a true mesh each person uploads a separate stream to everyone else — N×(N−1) streams — and a laptop's uplink dies around four or five people. So real products use a Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU): each client uploads one stream, and the SFU forwards the right streams (and the right simulcast/SVC layers) to each receiver.

the realization

The SFU still speaks WebRTC. To each client it looks exactly like a peer — doing ICE, DTLS, SRTP, RTCP. Everything on this page is still the substrate; the SFU is just a very well-connected, very smart peer in a datacenter. (An older design, the MCU, decodes and re-mixes everyone into one stream — simple clients, brutal server cost, rarely used now.)

And the sentence that ties it all together, now earned: WebRTC gives you nothing for free and re-solves everything UDP threw away — but only as much as live media actually needs, and no more.
